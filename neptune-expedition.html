    About this expedition

    What is it like to fly inside Neptune’s storms?

    Fly through Neptune’s storm layer in an imagined probe, with a real NASA 3D model of Voyager 2 in formation. White methane cirrus streams across a deep-blue cloud deck, and a dark vortex inspired by the Great Dark Spot turns on the horizon — five discoveries in the windiest atmosphere in the Solar System.

    Neptune is the most distant planet, about 4.5 billion km from the Sun, where sunlight is roughly 900 times dimmer than on Earth. Even so, its winds are the fastest measured on any planet — up to about 2,000 km/h. When Voyager 2 flew past in August 1989 it photographed the Great Dark Spot, a storm about as wide as Earth; by 1994 the Hubble Space Telescope showed it had vanished. Neptune’s largest moon, Triton, orbits backwards and was probably captured from the Kuiper Belt.

    Five discoveries to find

    1. The storm vista — cirrus lanes streaming toward the dark vortex.
    2. A cirrus wisp — a methane ice crystal in the specimen viewer.
    3. The storm wall — the edge of a storm wider than Earth.
    4. The wind shear line — where Neptune’s winds reverse direction.
    5. The storm spinner — a marker that shows how the vortex turns.

    How to explore

    Press Begin the flight. The probe cruises forward on its own; W A S D drift sideways, Space rises and C sinks toward the cloud deck. Drag to look around; on phones and tablets a touch pad appears. Press G to compare the local gravity with Earth’s, P for photo mode, and E to record a discovery in your journal — progress is saved on your own device.

    Science and artistic reconstruction

    Wind speeds, the Great Dark Spot and Triton are based on Voyager 2 and Hubble observations; Voyager 2 is a real NASA 3D model. The cloud deck, winds and vortex are artistic reconstructions — the flight is far gentler than real Neptunian weather.
