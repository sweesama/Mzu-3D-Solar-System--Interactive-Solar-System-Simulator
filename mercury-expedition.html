THE EXPEDITION FIELD GUIDE

Take the long way home.

This is a small, freely explorable cratered plain, not a whole-planet simulation. The five observation points are different views of the same place; two of them can only be reached on foot.

Follow the amber guide dots and distance arrow. Walk up to a stop and press E or Discover to add it to your journal. Quick travel does not automatically record a discovery.

Moving around

You are exploring on foot as an astronaut. Walk with W A S D or the arrow keys; Shift gives a brisker pace. Press Space to jump, then wait to land before jumping again. Momentum carries you forward in the air. Drag to look, including down at your boots. Touchscreens have direction and Jump buttons. H hides notes; P opens photo mode; Esc closes it.

What you are seeing

    Science meets imagination

    Mercury is the smallest planet and the closest to the Sun, yet its gravity matches Mars because it is mostly a giant iron core. Dayside reaches about 430°C and night drops to −180°C. Mariner 10 and MESSENGER mapped its cratered face, lobate scarps, and strange bright hollows. Terrain and observation points are procedural art, not survey data.

    Mercury has essentially no atmosphere — an exosphere too thin to carry sound. Outside the suit there is only silence; inside you hear the life-support fan and footfalls conducted through the body. Gravity is 3.7 m/s² — press G to compare with Earth.

    Mercury has no moons and no air to dim the stars. The huge Sun dominates; Earth and Venus shine as small points. Click a bright body to learn more.

    No external imagery on this page — terrain, rock textures, and the sky are generated locally in your browser.

    IN THE MERCURIAN SKY

    See it in the Solar System

    About this expedition

    What is it like to stand on Mercury?

    Walk an imagined cratered plain on Mercury — the smallest planet and the closest to the Sun — in first person. You move and jump in Mercury gravity, 3.7 m/s² and almost exactly the same as Mars, under a black sky where the Sun looks two to three times wider than it does from Earth. Look straight at it and your camera catches a lens flare.

    Mercury has no real atmosphere to trap heat or scatter light, so the sky is black at noon and temperatures swing from about 430 °C in daylight to −180 °C at night. As its iron core cooled, the whole planet shrank and its crust wrinkled into long cliffs called lobate scarps. It also has bright, shallow “hollows” found nowhere else in the Solar System.

    Quick facts

    Surface gravity
    3.7 m/s² — about 38% of Earth’s
    Distance from the Sun
    About 58 million km (0.39 AU)
    Length of a year
    88 Earth days
    Length of a day
    About 176 Earth days, sunrise to sunrise
    Temperature
    About 430 °C by day, −180 °C at night
    Spacecraft
    Mariner 10 (1974–75), MESSENGER (orbit 2011–2015)

    Five discoveries to find

    1. A mountain born of impact — the central peak of a large crater.
    2. A block thrown by impact — a sharp-edged ejecta block in the specimen viewer.
    3. A listener in the glare — a real NASA 3D model of MESSENGER (its placement here is imagined).
    4. Mercury’s hollows — bright pits that may form as rock loses volatile material.
    5. A wrinkle from a shrinking world — a lobate scarp raised as the planet contracted.

    How to explore

    Press Step onto Mercury, then walk with W A S D or the arrow keys, hold Shift to move faster, press Space to jump and drag to look around. On phones and tablets a touch pad appears. Press G to compare the local gravity with Earth’s, P for photo mode, and E to record a discovery in your journal — progress is saved on your own device.

    Science and artistic reconstruction

    The black sky, dark cratered ground, scarps and hollows are informed by Mariner 10 and MESSENGER imagery. The terrain, props and observation points are procedurally generated art, not survey data.
