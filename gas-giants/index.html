What they are made of

Jupiter and Saturn are mostly hydrogen and helium. Neither has a solid surface you could stand on; the atmosphere simply gets denser with depth. Saturn is the least dense planet. At 687 kg/m³ it sits below water, which is why people say it would float, if an ocean could hold it. Its rings are mostly water ice, about 270,000 km across and often only tens of metres thick.

Uranus and Neptune wear a hydrogen-helium atmosphere, with methane that takes out red light and leaves them blue-green and blue. Under the clouds, much of Uranus is thought to be a hot, dense fluid of water, methane and ammonia. Neptune is a little smaller than Uranus and more massive, and it carries the fastest winds described on this site, up to about 2,000 km/h. Uranus has 13 known rings, narrow and dark, and rolls around the Sun on its side, an axis tilt of about 97.8°. Neptune’s rings are faint.

Frequently asked questions

Which of these worlds is the largest?

Jupiter, 142,984 km across and 1,898 × 10²⁴ kg. Saturn is next in size. Neptune is smaller than Uranus but more massive: 102 against 86.8 × 10²⁴ kg.

What is the difference between a gas giant and an ice giant?

Jupiter and Saturn are gas giants, made mostly of hydrogen and helium, with no solid surface. Uranus and Neptune are ice giants: a hydrogen-helium atmosphere over a deep hot fluid of water, methane and ammonia.

How many moons do they have?

As of 2026, NASA Science lists 115 IAU-recognized moons for Jupiter and 293 confirmed moons for Saturn, as of August 2026. The NASA Planetary Fact Sheet still lists 95 and 274, and lists 28 for Uranus and 16 for Neptune. Counts change as new moons are confirmed.

Which has the brightest rings?

Saturn. Its main rings span roughly 270,000 km and are made mostly of water ice, often only tens of metres thick. Jupiter’s rings are faint and dusty. Uranus has 13 known rings, narrow and dark. Neptune’s rings are faint.

Is Saturn less dense than water?

Yes. The NASA Planetary Fact Sheet density used on this site is 687 kg per cubic metre, below water at about 1,000. There is no ocean large enough to test it.
