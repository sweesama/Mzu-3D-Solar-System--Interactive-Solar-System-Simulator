Which planets have rings?

Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune. All four giant planets have rings. Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars do not. Saturn’s are the ones you can see: a broad sheet of ice. The other three are faint, dark or extremely narrow.

Why Saturn’s rings are easy to see

NASA describes Saturn’s rings as pieces of comets, asteroids or shattered moons: billions of chunks of ice and rock, from dust grains to house-sized pieces, with a few as large as mountains. From the cloud tops they would look mostly white. The system extends up to 282,000 km from the planet, yet the main rings are typically only about 10 metres thick. The Cassini Division, the gap between the A and B rings, is about 4,700 km across.

That sheet reflects a lot of sunlight. On the NASA Saturn fact sheet, a smaller magnitude number is brighter. At opposition the globe alone is magnitude 0.7, and 0.05 once the rings are included. The maximum goes from 0.43 to −0.55. Jupiter the planet is still brighter in the sky than Saturn. Jupiter’s rings are not. NASA says they are small, dark particles, difficult to see except when backlit by the Sun. Voyager 1 found them in 1979. Galileo later found that the dust is knocked off Jupiter’s small inner moons by meteoroids.

Uranus is surrounded by 13 faint rings. NASA names them, outward from the planet: Zeta, 6, 5, 4, Alpha, Beta, Eta, Gamma, Delta, Lambda, Epsilon, Nu and Mu. The inner nine are mostly narrow and dark grey. Two outer rings are different: one reddish, like other dusty rings, and one blue, like Saturn’s E ring. Uranus itself is only just visible to excellent eyesight under a dark sky. Its mean opposition magnitude on the fact sheet is 5.57, so a set of dark threads does not become a second Saturn.

Neptune is not visible to the naked eye. It has at least five main rings and four prominent arcs. Outward from the planet they are Galle, Leverrier, Lassell, Arago and Adams. Galle and Leverrier are each about 15 km wide. Adams is under 50 km wide, and that is where the arcs Liberté, Egalité, Fraternité and Courage clump. NASA says the rings are thought to be relatively young and short-lived, and that the moon Galatea helps hold the arcs together. Voyager 2 photographed them in August 1989. A 15 km ring is a thread. The Cassini Division in Saturn’s rings is about 4,700 km across, hundreds of times that width, and Saturn’s icy sheet goes on for a vast distance beyond the gap.

Where Neptune’s rings sit

Bar length is distance from the centre of Neptune, using the kilometre figures on NASA’s Neptune facts page. The first bar is the planet’s equatorial radius from the NASA fact sheet, 24,764 km, so the rings are outside the globe. It is a diagram of those distances, not a photograph and not the 3D model. The rings themselves are far too narrow to draw: 15 km is a hairline on a chart that reaches 62,930 km.

Frequently asked questions

Which planets have rings?

Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune. The NASA Planetary Fact Sheet marks a ring system for each of them, and for none of Mercury, Venus, Earth or Mars.

Why are Saturn’s rings easy to see?

They are a broad disc of ice that NASA says would look mostly white, extending up to 282,000 km from the planet, while the main rings are typically only about 10 metres thick. On the NASA Saturn fact sheet, a smaller magnitude is brighter: at opposition the globe alone is magnitude 0.7, and 0.05 once the rings are included. Jupiter’s rings are small dark particles that are hard to see except when backlit. Uranus has 13 faint rings, the inner ones narrow and dark grey. Neptune’s main rings are only about 15 km wide, or under 50 km for the Adams ring.

Do Earth, Mercury, Venus or Mars have rings?

No. The NASA fact sheet lists planetary ring system “No” for Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars.

What does the 3D model on this page actually draw?

All four giant planets have a ring mesh in this site’s explorer. Saturn’s mesh uses the Cassini-based ring image, and those rings show clearly in the close view. Jupiter’s generated sheet is faint and nearly edge-on. Uranus and Neptune have generated discs too, but they are easy to miss at this camera distance, and they are not maps of the named rings. The orbital view is not to scale. Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars have no ring mesh.

Can I fly through the rings?

Saturn and Uranus have separate ring-plane flights. Jupiter’s expedition is a cloud descent, and Neptune’s is a storm flight. Those flights are artistic visits, not surveys.
