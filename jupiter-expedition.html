THE EXPEDITION FIELD GUIDE

There is no ground here.

This is a small, freely explorable column of atmosphere, not a whole-planet simulation. The five observation points are different views of the same descent; two of them lie deeper and can only be reached by sinking on your own.

Follow the amber guide dots and distance arrow. Drift to a stop and press E or Discover to add it to your journal. Quick travel does not automatically record a discovery.

Moving around

You are a descending probe — there is no surface to stand on. The probe sinks on its own; hold Space to fire the ascent thruster. W A S D or the arrow keys drift sideways; Shift drifts faster. Drag to look. Touchscreens have direction and Burn buttons. H hides notes; P opens photo mode; Esc closes it.

Disable camera drift for a steadier view.

What you are seeing

    Science meets imagination

    Jupiter has no surface — its hydrogen atmosphere thickens downward until it behaves like a hot metallic ocean. The Galileo probe descended into these clouds in 1995 and transmitted for about an hour. The ammonia cirrus, the great storm vortex, the deeper haze, and the lightning flashes are artistic reconstructions, not survey data.

    The probe sinks at a comfortable terminal-velocity drift — real descent profiles are far harsher. Press G to compare sink rates under Earth’s gentler gravity. Wind rush follows your descent rate; the static crackles honour Jupiter’s powerful radio emissions.

    Look up through the thinning haze — a Sun five times farther than from Earth is a small fierce disc, about 4% of Earth’s daylight. Click it to learn more.

    No external imagery on this page — clouds, the storm, and the sky are generated locally in your browser.

    About this expedition

    What would it be like to fall into Jupiter?

    Jupiter has no surface to stand on, so this expedition is an atmospheric descent. You ride an imagined probe down through the clouds of the largest planet — past white ammonia cirrus and the rim of a giant storm, into a dark layer lit by lightning — with five discoveries at five different depths, all in your browser.

    Jupiter is made mostly of hydrogen and helium. Its familiar stripes are three cloud decks — ammonia ice on top, ammonium hydrosulfide below and water clouds deeper still — stretched into bands by powerful winds and a day that lasts under ten hours. In 1995 NASA’s Galileo probe parachuted into these clouds and sent data for about an hour before heat and pressure silenced it.

    Quick facts

    Gravity at the cloud tops
    24.79 m/s² — about 2.5 times Earth’s
    Solid surface
    None — the atmosphere just gets denser
    Atmosphere
    About 90% hydrogen and 10% helium (by molecules)
    Length of a day
    About 9 hours 56 minutes
    Great Red Spot
    A storm wider than Earth, watched since at least the 1830s
    Probes
    Galileo entry probe (1995); Juno in orbit since 2016

    Five discoveries to find

    1. The veil of ammonia — the highest of Jupiter’s three cloud layers.
    2. A frozen crystal — an ammonia ice grain in the specimen viewer.
    3. The storm that outlives nations — the rim of a hurricane wider than Earth.
    4. Flash in the deep — lightning inside the water-driven storm layer.
    5. Where signals fade — the depth where heat and pressure end a probe’s mission.

    How to explore

    Press Begin descent. The probe sinks on its own; hold Space to fire the ascent thruster and use W A S D or the arrow keys to drift sideways. Drag to look around; on phones and tablets a touch pad appears. Press G to compare the local gravity with Earth’s, P for photo mode, and E to record a discovery in your journal — progress is saved on your own device.

    Science and artistic reconstruction

    The cloud layers, storm and lightning are based on what Galileo, Juno and telescopes have revealed; a real NASA 3D model of the Galileo orbiter hangs overhead at the start. The cloud shapes, depths and storm placement are artistic reconstructions, not survey data.
