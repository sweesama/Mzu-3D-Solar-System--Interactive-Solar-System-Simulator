How the numbers are computed

The Sun and planets use the Keplerian elements and rates JPL published for approximate positions over 3000 BC–3000 AD (E. M. Standish and J. G. Williams). The tighter 1800–2050 set ends in 2050, so it is not used. Earth is the Earth–Moon barycentre in that table. That point lies about 4,670 km from Earth’s centre, toward the Moon, which is small beside a planetary distance and is already inside JPL’s stated error for the fit.

JPL’s nominal distance error for this element set, across that whole historical span, is about 1,000 km for Mercury, 8,000 km for Venus, 15,000 km for the Earth–Moon barycentre, 30,000 km for Mars, 1 million km for Jupiter, 4 million km for Saturn, 8 million km for Uranus and 4 million km for Neptune. A given night is often better than the worst case. It is still not a Horizons ephemeris, and it is not the 3D explorer, whose starting angles are artistic.

The Moon is separate. Its distance is Earth’s centre to the Moon’s centre, from the series in Jean Meeus, Astronomical Algorithms, chapter 47. Light-time is distance divided by 299,792.458 km/s, using the astronomical unit of 149,597,870.7 km. “Next closest” is the next local minimum of that distance: perihelion, perigee, or the next planetary close approach.

Pluto is not in that JPL planet table. Its row uses the J2000 mean elements and centennial rates in the Explanatory Supplement to the Astronomical Almanac (1992, Table 5.8.1), a 250-year fit to DE200. The table allows about 2 million km for that approximate fit. It is not a Horizons ephemeris. Each body also has its own page, with kilometres, miles, astronomical units and light travel time.

Frequently asked questions

How far is each planet from Earth right now?

The table is centre-to-centre distance at the time shown. The Sun and planets use JPL’s approximate Keplerian elements for 3000 BC–3000 AD, with Earth represented by the Earth–Moon barycentre. The Moon’s distance is from Earth’s centre, using a series from Jean Meeus, Astronomical Algorithms. Pluto uses a separate 250-year Keplerian fit, described above, and is allowed about 2 million km. These are not JPL Horizons values.

What is light-time?

Light-time is the distance divided by the speed of light, 299,792.458 km/s. It is how long the light leaving that body takes to reach Earth. The positions themselves are not shifted by light-time.

What does approaching or receding mean?

The page compares the distance now with the distance one hour later. Approaching means the centre-to-centre gap is shrinking. It is the rate of change of distance, not the planet’s speed along its orbit.

What is the next closest date?

The next time this model’s distance reaches a local minimum: perihelion for the Sun, perigee for the Moon, and the next close approach for each planet. Outer-planet dates are softer, because JPL’s nominal distance error for this element set is millions of kilometres.

Why might these numbers differ from an almanac?

JPL’s nominal distance error for this 3000 BC–3000 AD element set runs from about 1,000 km for Mercury to several million kilometres for the outer planets. The Earth–Moon barycentre also sits about 4,670 km from Earth’s centre. The 3D explorer does not use these positions.

Can I see the planets in 3D?

Yes. Open the 3D explorer or a planet’s own page. That view is artistic: its starting angles are not this live calculation.
