THE EXPEDITION FIELD GUIDE

Take the long way home.

This is a small, freely explorable volcanic plain, not a whole-planet simulation. The five observation points are different views of the same place; two of them can only be reached on foot.

Follow the amber guide dots and distance arrow. Walk up to a stop and press E or Discover to add it to your journal. Quick travel does not automatically record a discovery.

Moving around

You are exploring on foot as an astronaut. Walk with W A S D or the arrow keys; Shift gives a brisker pace. Press Space to jump, then wait to land before jumping again. Momentum carries you forward in the air. Drag to look, including down at your boots. Touchscreens have direction and Jump buttons. H hides notes; P opens photo mode; Esc closes it.

Disable head motion for a steadier view.
Same takeoff push, different fall. Venus gravity is 8.87 m/s² — about 91% of Earth’s, so a jump feels almost like home.
Inside the suit: fan hum and conducted footfalls. Outside, dense air carries a low rumble, slow wind swells, and rare distant thunder. M toggles.

What you are seeing

    Science meets imagination

    Venus hides its surface under thick sulfuric-acid clouds; sunlight arrives as a dim, directionless orange glow. The Soviet Venera landers photographed flat, dark, slab-like basalt plains. Terrain and observation points are procedural art, not survey data.

    Venus is nearly Earth's twin in gravity: 8.87 m/s² — press G to feel how small the difference is. Its atmosphere is 92 times denser than Earth's and carries sound far better: a low rumble, slow heavy wind swells, and rare muffled thunder. Inside the suit you hear the life-support fan and footfalls conducted through the body.

    Venus has no moons, and its clouds hide every other body — only the Sun’s brighter patch of haze is clickable. Click it to learn more.

    No external imagery on this page — terrain, rock textures, and the sky are generated locally in your browser.

    About this expedition

    What is it like on the surface of Venus?

    Stand on an imagined volcanic plain on Venus — the hottest planet in the Solar System — in first person. You walk in Venus gravity, 8.87 m/s² or about 90% of Earth’s, beneath a heavy orange sky, past a lava channel, a pancake dome and fractured basalt slabs like those photographed by the Soviet Venera landers.

    Venus is almost Earth’s twin in size but nothing like it at the surface. Its carbon-dioxide atmosphere is so dense that surface pressure is about 92 times Earth’s — similar to being nearly a kilometre under the ocean — and a runaway greenhouse effect keeps the ground near 465 °C, hot enough to melt lead. Sulfuric-acid clouds hide the Sun completely, so daylight is a dim, directionless glow.

    Quick facts

    Surface gravity
    8.87 m/s² — about 90% of Earth’s
    Surface temperature
    About 465 °C, day and night
    Surface pressure
    About 92 times Earth’s
    Atmosphere
    About 96.5% CO₂, with sulfuric-acid clouds
    Rotation
    243 Earth days per turn — and it spins backwards
    Longest surface mission
    Venera 13, 127 minutes (1982)

    Five discoveries to find

    1. A floor of broken slabs — fractured volcanic plates like those in Venera photographs.
    2. Basalt in orange light — a rotatable rock specimen, grey under an amber sky.
    3. A river that was lava — a winding channel carved by lava, not water.
    4. A visitor that lasted 127 minutes — an artistic tribute to the Venera 13 lander.
    5. A dome of thick lava — a “pancake dome” formed by sticky, slow-moving lava.

    How to explore

    Press Step onto Venus, then walk with W A S D or the arrow keys, hold Shift to move faster, press Space to jump and drag to look around. On phones and tablets a touch pad appears. Press G to compare the local gravity with Earth’s, P for photo mode, and E to record a discovery in your journal — progress is saved on your own device.

    Science and artistic reconstruction

    The orange light, slab-like basalt and landforms are inspired by Venera surface photographs and by NASA’s Magellan radar maps (1990–1994); a real NASA 3D model of Magellan circles overhead. The lander is an artistic replica, and the terrain is procedurally generated art, not survey data.
