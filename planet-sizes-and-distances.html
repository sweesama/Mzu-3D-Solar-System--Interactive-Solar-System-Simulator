Planet sizes and distances compared

The planets differ enormously in size, and the distances between them are far larger than most pictures suggest. The tables below use NASA reference values; the 3D explorer can show the same proportions with its Real sizes, Real distances and True scale settings.

A solar system scale model: the Sun as a 1-metre ball

The Sun is about 1,391,400 km across. Shrink it to a ball 1 metre wide and keep every other size and distance in proportion:

Earth becomes a 9 mm bead on the far side of a football pitch, Jupiter is a 10 cm ball, about grapefruit-sized, 560 m away, and Neptune sits over 3 km from the ball. That is why the explorer’s default view compresses distances: at true scale, the planets would be invisible dots.

What stands out

  • The inner planets are close together and small. Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars all orbit within about 1.5 AU and their diameters added together are only about a quarter of Jupiter’s.
  • The gaps grow quickly. The distance roughly doubles from Jupiter (about 5.2 AU) to Saturn (9.6 AU) to Uranus (19.2 AU), and Neptune lies at about 30 AU.
  • Years get very long. Mercury circles the Sun in 88 days; Neptune needs about 164 years.
  • Venus and Earth are near twins in size, while Uranus and Neptune are a similar pair of ice giants about four times wider than Earth.

Frequently asked questions

Which planet is the biggest?

Jupiter. Its equatorial diameter is about 142,984 km, roughly 11 times Earth’s.

Which planet is the smallest?

Mercury, with a diameter of about 4,879 km, about 38% of Earth’s.

Why are solar system models not to scale?

At true scale the planets are tiny compared with the gaps between them. If the Sun were a 1-metre ball, Earth would be a 9 mm bead about 108 m away and Neptune would be about 3.2 km away, so a scale model rarely fits on a page or a screen.

How long does sunlight take to reach each planet?

About 3.2 minutes to Mercury, 8.3 minutes to Earth and about 4.2 hours to Neptune.
